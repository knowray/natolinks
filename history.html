Background depth

NATO’s founding bargain

The argument over NATO 3.0 starts in 1949: mutual assistance required more than a pledge. It required Allies to sustain the capacity to resist an attack.

Article 3 + Article 5

The treaty contains both capacity and solidarity.

Article 5 is the famous commitment that an armed attack on one Ally is considered an attack on all. But Article 3 requires Allies, separately and jointly, to maintain and develop their capacity to resist armed attack. The original model was shared capability plus mutual assistance—not a blank cheque from Washington.

Primary context: NATO on collective defence, Articles 3 and 5.

Eisenhower

The first SACEUR’s warning

As NATO’s first Supreme Allied Commander Europe, Dwight Eisenhower helped create the integrated military structure. Hegseth invoked his 1951 view that an indefinitely permanent U.S. troop presence would indicate failure of the European-defence project. The useful historical reading is not “America should leave”; it is that an American presence was meant to make European defence credible while Europe rebuilt real capacity.

The founding generation

Seven people, one unresolved question

Truman & Acheson

American architects of a durable transatlantic pledge, designed to deter Soviet pressure without abandoning European partners.

Bevin, Schuman & Spaak

European advocates of collective security who also understood that European recovery, integration and national force-building mattered.

Eisenhower & Ismay

Builders of NATO’s military and institutional machinery: deterrence requires plans, forces, interoperability and political cohesion.

The editorial question

Is NATO 3.0 an attempt to restore the balance that Article 3 always required—or does a reduced U.S. conventional presence risk weakening the deterrence that makes Article 5 credible? NATOlinks does not pre-judge the answer. It follows the evidence.
