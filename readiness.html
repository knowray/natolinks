Readiness atlas · working framework

Readiness is a system, not a number.

Alliance readiness is often described through troop totals or expenditure targets. Those matter, but they hide the real question: can forces detect, decide, move, fight, replenish and remain connected under pressure? This atlas makes the dependencies visible without claiming access to classified readiness data.

How to use this page

Look for the weakest link in the chain.

Public reporting can establish whether a country is buying a system, building a facility or committing a unit. It usually cannot verify detailed stocks, deployment plans or operational availability. NATOlinks therefore reads open material against seven connected systems. A favourable signal in one system does not cancel a serious constraint in another.

For example, a modern air-defence launcher is only part of readiness. The result also depends on sensors, command links, trained crews, interceptor depth, protected sites, repair, power, data integrity and the ability to move reloads under threat.

Seven connected systems

01 · Posture

Deterrence and force posture

Forward presence, trained formations, reserve mobilisation, pre-positioned equipment and the ability to reinforce at speed create the visible backbone of collective defence. The test is not simply how many units exist on paper, but whether they have routes, protection, fuel, ammunition and authority to move.

Public signals to watch: recurring exercises, permanent command arrangements, reception sites, readiness notices and sustainment agreements.

02 · Shield

Integrated air and missile defence

IAMD is a layered network of sensors, data, command-and-control, aircraft, ground-based systems, interceptors and logistics. It must defend both forces and the infrastructure that allows those forces to arrive and operate.

Public signals to watch: common command links, radar coverage, interceptor contracts, reload capacity, protected basing and multinational exercises.

03 · Depth

Munitions and industrial depth

Deterrence has a production dimension. A force must be able to replace ammunition, interceptors, drones, spares and damaged equipment during a sustained crisis. Production lines, skilled labour, supply chains, testing and financing are strategic infrastructure.

Public signals to watch: multi-year orders, jointly procured demand, expanded facilities, subcontractor capacity and transparent delivery milestones.

04 · Movement

Mobility, access and reception

Reinforcement depends on ports, rail, roads, bridges, airfields, border procedures, host-nation support, fuel and repair. Mobility is the conversion layer between national force structure and collective defence.

Public signals to watch: corridor upgrades, access agreements, cross-border exercises, logistics hubs and hardening of transport infrastructure.

05 · Nervous system

ISR, data and command

Awareness and decision advantage rely on sensors, secure communications, headquarters, shared operational pictures and the ability to keep systems running through cyber or electromagnetic disruption. Integration is frequently more decisive than the individual platform.

Public signals to watch: resilient networks, interoperable standards, command-post exercises, satellite and ISR programmes, electronic-warfare resilience.

06 · Approaches

Maritime and undersea security

The Atlantic, High North, Baltic and Black Sea connect North America and Europe, enable trade and reinforcement, and host critical cables and energy infrastructure. Maritime readiness includes anti-submarine warfare, mine countermeasures, surveillance, port security and repair capacity.

Public signals to watch: patrol patterns, maritime exercises, undersea monitoring, mine-warfare capability and port resilience.

07 · Endurance

Resilience and civil preparedness

Military force depends on civilian systems: power, transport, telecommunications, health care, finance, food and public confidence. Resilience is the ability to absorb disruption, keep essential services operating and support allied reinforcement.

Public signals to watch: continuity planning, infrastructure protection, stockpiles, cyber recovery, emergency exercises and civil–military coordination.

Regional lens

Different geographies, different friction.

The North and Baltic region centre on maritime access, air defence, reinforcement routes and harsh-environment sustainment. The eastern flank emphasises early warning, forward defence and the capacity to move heavy forces. The Black Sea and southeastern approaches bring maritime, air, energy and political-access constraints. The Atlantic remains the strategic bridge between North America and Europe.

Country dossiers should be read in that regional context—not as isolated national scorecards.

What the NATO 3.0 transition requires

European responsibility means usable systems at scale.

The strategic shift is not a slogan about strategic autonomy. It is a practical division of labour: European allies take much greater responsibility for conventional defence, reinforcement and industrial depth; the United States remains decisive in the alliance’s deterrent posture, high-end enabling capabilities and political weight. Progress should be measured by fielded, integrated and sustainable capability—not only announced ambition.
