Defence Industry

Where the Alliance’s capacity is actually made

NATOlinks follows the industrial bridge between a government commitment and a usable military capability: prime contractor, critical suppliers, production bottleneck, test/acceptance process and delivered system.

The production question

Procurement is not production.

A procurement announcement creates an intention; it does not create a factory slot, a trained workforce, energetic materials, components, qualification testing or stock in a unit. The industry section is designed to show each link in that chain.

For each programme, NATOlinks will identify the buying country, contract status, industrial partners, production location when public, stated delivery schedule, reported bottlenecks and the capability the programme is intended to provide.

Editorial rule

Company pages will distinguish company claims, government contract awards and independently corroborated delivery evidence. Inclusion is not endorsement; suppliers do not receive an implied NATO affiliation.

Land systems & ammunition

Rheinmetall, KNDS, BAE Systems, Nammo

European land-force capacity depends on more than platforms. Artillery rounds, propellants, explosives, barrels, spares, engines and repair all create different production timelines. The launch coverage will map public expansion plans and the procurement programmes that underpin them.

Questions: What is contracted? Where are inputs constrained? Is output for national stock, Ukraine support or both? What is the delivery date?

Air, missiles & sensors

MBDA, Thales, Hensoldt, Leonardo, Saab, Kongsberg

Air and missile defence spans radars, battle-management systems, interceptors, launchers, seekers, communications and integration services. No single firm or country delivers the whole architecture; interoperability and capacity are core issues.

Questions: Which layer is being built? What interceptor inventory and replacement flow is public? How does the system connect with allied sensors and command networks?

Maritime & undersea

Shipyards, naval electronics & munitions

North Atlantic, Baltic, Arctic and North Sea security depend on ship availability, anti-submarine capability, undersea awareness, missiles and shipyard throughput. Maritime industry also faces long lead times, skilled-labour constraints and complex supply chains.

Questions: Which hulls are available? How quickly can they be maintained? What protects ports, cables and sea lines of communication?

Dual-use & rapid technology

Software, drones and counter-drone

The fastest-moving part of the industrial landscape is not always the traditional prime. Smaller European firms can matter in autonomy, sensors, electronic warfare, software and uncrewed systems—but military adoption, standards and scale remain hard problems.

Questions: Can a company move from a demonstration to certified, supported, large-scale field use? Who owns sustainment and software assurance?

Mobility & sustainment

Trucks, rail, depots, repair & fuel

Military mobility is partly an industrial story. Heavy transport, recovery vehicles, rail loading, maintenance, secure warehousing and fuel distribution determine whether formations can reach and remain in theatre.

Questions: Which civilian suppliers are critical? What has been contracted? Where are the repair and spares bottlenecks?

Sensors, space & data

Radar, satellites, communications & electronic warfare

Europe needs a dependable sensor-to-decision chain. The public record should distinguish sensors ordered, satellites launched, networks available, software integrated and services actually accessible to military users.

Questions: Who provides sovereign or allied access? What is protected against interference? What is interoperable across nations?

Energetics & components

Propellants, explosives, seekers and microelectronics

The limiting factor is frequently not the visible prime contractor but a specialised upstream component. Energetic materials, rocket motors, seekers, optics and electronics shape the possible production rate.

Questions: Which inputs are single-source? Where can capacity expand? Which dependencies sit outside allied control?
